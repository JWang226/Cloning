\large Hybrid-measure fidelity and mass at infinity}

\begin{abstract}
Lemma C.1 has two purposes.  First, it extends the usual root fidelity of
quantum states to classical--quantum states whose classical part is a Radon
measure.  Second, it shows that probability mass escaping to infinity during
a weak-* limit has zero fidelity with a normalized target that remains in the
original space.  This note develops the background needed for those two
claims and then explains the proof.
\end{abstract}

\section{The picture before the formalism}

For a finite classical register, a classical--quantum positive operator has
the block-diagonal form
\[
  X=\bigoplus_i X_i,\qquad Y=\bigoplus_i Y_i,
\]
where the $X_i,Y_i$ are positive trace-class operators.  Orthogonal classical
blocks do not interfere, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-cq}
  \B(X,Y)=\sum_i \B(X_i,Y_i),
  \qquad
  \B(R,S):=\Tr\sqrt{R^{1/2}SR^{1/2}}.
\end{equation}
Lemma C.1 is the continuous version of \eqref{eq:finite-cq}: the sum over $i$
becomes an integral over a classical parameter $y\in\Hc$.

The only additional difficulty is noncompactness.  A sequence of probability
measures may move farther and farther away and have no mass left on any fixed
compact subset.  Compactness is recovered by adjoining an abstract point at
infinity.  The second part of Lemma C.1 says that mass stored at this new point
is orthogonal to a target supported on the original classical space.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Compact and trace-class operators}

Let $\F$ be the separable multimode Fock space.  We use
\[
  \K(\F)=\{\text{compact operators on }\F\},
  \qquad
  \T(\F)=\{\text{trace-class operators on }\F\}.
\]
The trace class is the Banach dual of the compact operators,
$\K(\F)^*=\T(\F)$, with pairing
\[
  \langle R,A\rangle=\Tr(RA),
  \qquad R\in\T(\F),\quad A\in\K(\F).
\]
The mass of a positive element $R\in\T(\F)$ is $\Tr R$; those with trace at
most one are possibly subnormalized quantum states.  If $R$ and $S$ are
positive, the formula defining $\B(R,S)$ above remains valid without any
normalization assumption.

\subsection{The algebra of continuous quantum-valued test functions}

Let $\Hc$ be the finite-dimensional classical parameter space in the paper.
The relevant nonunital $C^*$-algebra is
\[
  \A=C_0\bigl(\Hc;\K(\F)\bigr).
\]
Thus $A\in\A$ is a norm-continuous compact-operator-valued function $y\mapsto
A(y)$ which vanishes in operator norm as $\lVert y\rVert\to\infty$.

The vector-valued Riesz representation theorem identifies $C_0(X;E)^*$ with
finite regular $E^*$-valued measures on $X$.  Since
$\K(\F)^*=\T(\F)$, every $\Xi\in\A^*$ is therefore represented by a finite
$\T(\F)$-valued Radon measure through
\[
  \Xi(A)=\int_{\Hc}\Tr[A(y)\,\Xi(\dd y)].
\]
If $\Xi$ is a positive functional, then $\Xi(E)\geq0$ for every Borel set
$E$, so the representing measure is positive.  Its scalar trace measure is
\[
  \mu_\Xi(E):=\Tr\Xi(E).
\]
If $\lambda$ dominates $\mu_\Xi$, the operator-valued Radon--Nikodym theorem
gives a measurable density $X_y\in\T(\F)_+$ such that
\[
  \Xi(E)=\int_E X_y\,\dd\lambda(y),
  \qquad
  \Xi(A)=\int_{\Hc}\Tr\!\left[X_yA(y)\right]\dd\lambda(y).
\]
Here one uses that $\T(\F)$ has the Radon--Nikodym property; separability of
the Fock space is part of the standard setting.
For two positive functionals $\Xi$ and $\Upsilon$, one may always take
\[
  \lambda=\mu_\Xi+\mu_\Upsilon.
\]
They then have densities $X_y$ and $Y_y$ relative to the same scalar measure.
This is the continuous analogue of writing two block-diagonal operators using
the same list of classical blocks.  These densities are positive trace-class
weights, not necessarily normalized states.  If
$X_y=p_y\rho_y$ and $Y_y=q_y\sigma_y$, with $\rho_y,\sigma_y$ normalized, then
\[
  \B(X_y,Y_y)=\sqrt{p_yq_y}\,\B(\rho_y,\sigma_y).
\]
Thus the integrand combines the classical Hellinger overlap
$\sqrt{p_yq_y}$ with the conditional quantum fidelity.

The Bochner space $L^1(\Hc;\T(\F))$ used in the main text embeds into
$\A^*$, but $\A^*$ is larger: it also contains measures singular with respect
to Lebesgue measure, such as point masses.  This larger space is needed because
weak limits of ordinary densities need not remain ordinary densities.

\subsection{Transition amplitude}

For density operators on a Hilbert space, $\B(R,S)$ is the root fidelity.
Uhlmann's theorem identifies it with the largest overlap between purifications
of $R$ and $S$.  The Alberti--Uhlmann transition amplitude extends the same
notion to arbitrary positive functionals on a $C^*$-algebra.  Concretely,
\[
  \B_\A(\phi,\psi)
  =\sup_{\pi,\xi,\eta}|\langle\xi,\eta\rangle|,
\]
where $\pi$ is a common representation and $\xi,\eta$ implement $\phi,\psi$ in
that representation.  The facts needed here are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item on trace-class operators it equals
  $\Tr\sqrt{R^{1/2}SR^{1/2}}$;
  \item it is additive across orthogonal central direct summands;
  \item it is separately homogeneous:
  $\B(aR,bS)=\sqrt{ab}\,\B(R,S)$ for $a,b\geq0$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unitization and the point at infinity}

Because $\A$ has no identity, its unitization is
\[
  \At=\A\oplus\C I.
\]
Every element is written $A+tI$, and the quotient
$\At/\A\cong\C$ has the character
\[
  \chi_\infty(A+tI)=t.
\]
It is useful to regard $\chi_\infty$ as evaluation at an abstract point at
infinity.  If $\Upsilon$ is normalized on $\A$, its canonical state extension
is
\[
  \widehat\Upsilon(A+tI)=\Upsilon(A)+t.
\]

The bidual $\At^{**}$ is a von Neumann algebra.  The ideal $\A$ has a central
support projection $e\in\At^{**}$.  It separates the two mutually orthogonal
parts
\[
  e\At^{**}\quad\text{(the original space)},
  \qquad
  (1-e)\At^{**}\quad\text{(the quotient at infinity)}.
\]
Restricting a state on $\At$ to $\A$ keeps its $e$-part and discards its
$(1-e)$-part.  The trace defect of the restriction is precisely the possible
mass at infinity.  For example, $e\widehat\Xi$ denotes the functional
$b\mapsto\widehat\Xi(eb)$ on the $e$-summand.

\section{Statement of Lemma C.1}

\begin{lemmaC}
Let positive functionals $\Xi,\Upsilon\in\A^*$ have densities $X_y,Y_y$
relative to a common dominating measure $\lambda$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hybrid-fid}
  \B(\Xi,\Upsilon)
  =\int_{\Hc}\Tr\sqrt{X_y^{1/2}Y_yX_y^{1/2}}\,\dd\lambda(y).
\end{equation}
The integral is independent of the chosen dominating measure and equals the
Alberti--Uhlmann transition amplitude on $\A$.

Now let $\widehat\Xi$ be a state on $\At$, let
$\Xi=\widehat\Xi|_\A$, and let $\Upsilon$ be normalized on $\A$.  For the
canonical extension $\widehat\Upsilon$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinity}
  \B_{\At}(\widehat\Xi,\widehat\Upsilon)
  =\B_\A(\Xi,\Upsilon).
\end{equation}
\end{lemmaC}

\section{Why the fidelity integral is correct}

\subsection{Independence of the dominating measure}

Suppose $\lambda=f\nu$.  Densities relative to $\nu$ are $f(y)X_y$ and
$f(y)Y_y$.  Fiberwise homogeneity gives
\[
  \B\bigl(f(y)X_y,f(y)Y_y\bigr)
  =f(y)\B(X_y,Y_y).
\]
Consequently,
\[
  \int\B(fX,fY)\,\dd\nu
  =\int\B(X,Y)\,\dd\lambda.
\]
The same argument after passing to a common refinement handles any two
dominating measures.  Thus the right side of \eqref{eq:hybrid-fid} depends only
on $\Xi$ and $\Upsilon$.

\subsection{The direct-integral argument}

The Hilbert--Schmidt class is
\[
  \mathcal T_2(\F)
  :=\{Z\in\mathcal B(\F):\lVert Z\rVert_2^2
  :=\Tr(Z^*Z)<\infty\},
  \qquad \langle Z,W\rangle_2:=\Tr(Z^*W).
\]
It is the standard Hilbert space for the algebra $\K(\F)$.  Since
$X_y^{1/2}$ and $Y_y^{1/2}$ are Hilbert--Schmidt, they define
square-integrable fields in
\[
  \int_{\Hc}^{\oplus}\mathcal T_2(\F)\,\dd\lambda(y).
\]
More precisely, $\A$ has the representation
\[
  (\pi(A)Z)_y=A(y)Z_y,
  \qquad
  \lVert A(y)Z_y\rVert_2\leq\lVert A(y)\rVert\lVert Z_y\rVert_2.
\]
Thus ``fiberwise'' means that $A(y)$ acts by left multiplication at each
$y$, without mixing different values of $y$.  The square-root field
$X^{1/2}:y\mapsto X_y^{1/2}$ implements $\Xi$ since
\[
  \bigl\langle X^{1/2},\pi(A)X^{1/2}\bigr\rangle
  =\int_{\Hc}\Tr[X_yA(y)]\,\dd\lambda(y)=\Xi(A),
\]
and the same identity with $Y$ gives $\Upsilon$.

Uhlmann's theorem applied in each fiber says that the largest possible
overlap at $y$ is
\[
  \Tr\sqrt{X_y^{1/2}Y_yX_y^{1/2}}.
\]
The polar partial isometries can be chosen measurably as a function of $y$.
After a fixed amplification if necessary (equivalently, by using measurable
$\varepsilon$-maximizers), the pointwise choices form an admissible Uhlmann
field.  Integrating their overlaps proves that the transition amplitude is at
least the right side of \eqref{eq:hybrid-fid}.  Conversely, the fiberwise
Uhlmann bound applies to every pair of implementing fields.  More formally,
$C_0(\Hc;\K(\F))$ is type I, so every common representation disintegrates over
$\Hc$ into multiples of the defining representation of $\K(\F)$.  The local
bound therefore controls every global overlap.  This proves equality.

The argument is exactly the continuous form of the finite-block identity
\eqref{eq:finite-cq}.  The direct-integral language is needed only to justify
that the fiberwise optimizations combine into one representation of the
functionals.

\section{Why mass at infinity contributes nothing}

The central projection $e$ gives orthogonal decompositions
\[
  \widehat\Xi=e\widehat\Xi+(1-e)\widehat\Xi,
  \qquad
  \widehat\Upsilon=e\widehat\Upsilon+(1-e)\widehat\Upsilon.
\]
Because $\Upsilon$ already has total mass one on $\A$, its canonical extension
has no mass left for the quotient:
\[
  (1-e)\widehat\Upsilon=0.
\]
Indeed, for an approximate identity $(a_i)$ of $\A$, one has
$a_i\to e$ weak-* and
$\widehat\Upsilon(e)=\lim_i\Upsilon(a_i)=\|\Upsilon\|=1$.
Additivity of transition amplitude over the two central summands now gives
\begin{align*}
  \B_{\At}(\widehat\Xi,\widehat\Upsilon)
  &=\B(e\widehat\Xi,e\widehat\Upsilon)
    +\B((1-e)\widehat\Xi,0)\\
  &=\B(e\widehat\Xi,e\widehat\Upsilon).
\end{align*}
The last term is precisely the transition amplitude of the restrictions
$\Xi$ and $\Upsilon$ to $\A$: one uses
$e\At^{**}\cong\A^{**}$ and invariance of transition amplitude under normal
extension to the bidual.  This proves \eqref{eq:infinity}.

Notice that the lemma does not say the escaping mass disappears from
$\widehat\Xi$.  It remains a genuine component of that state, but it lies in
a central sector where the target is zero, so its contribution to fidelity is
zero.

\subsection*{A classical example}

Take $\Hc=\mathbb R$ and ignore the quantum fiber.  Let the target be the point
mass $\Upsilon=\delta_0$, and consider on the one-point compactification
\[
  \widehat\Xi=(1-a)\delta_0+a\delta_\infty,
  \qquad 0\leq a\leq1.
\]
Its restriction to $C_0(\mathbb R)$ is $\Xi=(1-a)\delta_0$.  Classical root
fidelity gives
\[
  \B(\widehat\Xi,\widehat\Upsilon)
  =\sqrt{1-a}
  =\B(\Xi,\Upsilon).
\]
The $a$ units of mass at infinity pair with zero target mass and hence add
nothing.  Similarly, $\delta_n$ converges to $\delta_\infty$ in the
unitization as $n\to\infty$, while its restriction converges vaguely to zero.

\section{Why the paper needs Lemma C.1}

In the F\o lner compactness argument following the lemma, averaged channels
are extracted by weak-* compactness on the unitized algebra.  A limiting
output need not have an ordinary Bochner density: it may have a singular
classical component, and some mass may escape to infinity.  Lemma C.1 supplies
the two facts needed to pass the fidelity payoff to that limit:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item fidelity of the remaining classical--quantum measure is computed by
  the intrinsic fiberwise integral \eqref{eq:hybrid-fid};
  \item any quotient mass created at infinity has zero overlap with the
  normalized target, by \eqref{eq:infinity}.
\end{enumerate}
This is why the lemma is formulated using positive functionals and Radon
measures rather than only ordinary $L^1$ densities.

\section{Minimal prerequisite checklist}

To follow the first half of the lemma, it is enough to know root fidelity,
Uhlmann's theorem, Radon--Nikodym densities, and the finite classical--quantum
block formula \eqref{eq:finite-cq}.  The direct-integral formalism makes the
continuous version rigorous.

The second half additionally uses three standard operator-algebra ideas:
unitization of a nonunital $C^*$-algebra, the bidual as a von Neumann algebra,
and decomposition by a central projection supporting an ideal.  Its content
is nevertheless simple: a target of total mass one in the original space has
zero component at infinity, so an escaping component of the other state is
orthogonal to it.
