\title{A Short Guide to Proposition C.3\\
\large The multimode least-noise moment}
\author{}

\begin{abstract}
Proposition C.3 says that the quantum-limited amplifier maximizes every
product of nonincreasing photon-number tests among displacement-covariant
amplifiers of the same gain.  It allows a trace-nonincreasing map and an
arbitrary multimode map that may couple the modes.  The proof first reduces
to the one-mode stochastic-order theorem of Gu\c{t}\u{a} and Matsumoto, then
turns the other modes into weighted environments and iterates the one-mode
bound.  This note explains the normalization, phase twirl, stochastic order,
and tensorization argument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Informal meaning}

Fix an intensity gain $\gamma>1$.  A displacement-covariant amplifier must
send an input displacement $z$ to the amplified displacement
$\sqrt\gamma z$, but it may add different amounts of noise.  The
quantum-limited amplifier, whose idler is in the vacuum, adds the least
possible noise.  After phase twirling, a displacement-covariant amplifier
can be represented by the same two-mode-squeezing interaction with different
phase-invariant idler states.  Photons already present in the idler create
additional stimulated output photons; choosing the vacuum removes this avoidable
contribution, leaving only the spontaneous noise required by amplification.
The least-noise theorem strengthens this mean-noise intuition by showing that
the entire vacuum-idler photon-number law is smallest in stochastic order.

For a phase-invariant output state $\rho$ with photon-number probabilities
$P_k=\langle k|\rho|k\rangle$, the expectation of a number test is
\[
  \Tr[\rho\,w(\widehat N)]=\sum_{k\ge0}P_k w(k).
\]
If $w$ is nonincreasing, an outcome with fewer photons receives a larger score;
for example, $w(k)=\boldsymbol 1_{\{k\le K\}}$ measures the probability of a
low-energy outcome, while $w(k)=e^{-sk}$ smoothly discounts high photon
numbers.  Moving probability mass toward higher photon numbers can only
decrease such an expectation.  Hence the least-noise output, whose
photon-number law is stochastically smallest, has the largest expectation of
$w(\widehat N)$ among the admissible amplifiers.  In several modes,
Proposition C.3 proves the corresponding statement for product tests
\[
  W(\widehat{\mathbf N})
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^s w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell).
\]
The competitor $\Gamma$ need not factor over the modes; the proof is designed
to handle correlations created by a joint channel.

\section{Thermal inputs and the reference amplifier}

On one bosonic mode, the thermal state with parameter $q\in(0,1)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thermal}
  \tau_q=(1-q)\sum_{k\geq0}q^k\ketbra{k}.
\end{equation}
Its photon-number distribution is geometric.  At gain $\gamma$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma}
  q^{(\gamma)}=1-\frac{1-q}{\gamma}.
\end{equation}
The quantum-limited amplifier maps
\[
  \tau_q\longmapsto\tau_{q^{(\gamma)}}.
\]
Since
\[
  q^{(\gamma)}-q
  =\frac{(\gamma-1)(1-q)}{\gamma}>0,
\]
the output geometric law is broader than the input law, as amplification
requires.

For $s$ modes, choose $q_1,\ldots,q_s\in(0,1)$ and write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product-thermal}
  \tau=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^s\tau_{q_\ell},
  \qquad
  \tau^{(\gamma)}
  =\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^s\tau_{q_\ell^{(\gamma)}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Decreasing tests and stochastic order}

A probability law $P$ on $\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$ stochastically dominates $Q$
if
\[
  \sum_k f(k)P_k\geq\sum_k f(k)Q_k
\]
for every bounded increasing function $f$.  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decreasing-test}
  \sum_k w(k)P_k\leq\sum_k w(k)Q_k
\end{equation}
for every bounded nonnegative nonincreasing function $w$.
Equivalently, writing $Q\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}P$,
\[
  \sum_{k=0}^m Q_k\geq\sum_{k=0}^m P_k
  \qquad(m\geq0).
\]

The direction is easy to remember: the stochastically larger law places more
mass at high photon numbers, so it scores less under a test that rewards low
photon numbers.  Proposition C.3 uses this scalar order for the
photon-number distributions of phase-invariant states; it does not assert an
operator inequality between density matrices.

\section{Statement of Proposition C.3}

\begin{propC}
Let $\Gamma$ be a completely positive trace-nonincreasing map on the
$s$-mode trace class satisfying
\[
  \Gamma(D(z)XD(z)^*)
  =D(\sqrt\gamma z)\Gamma(X)D(\sqrt\gamma z)^*
  \qquad(z\in\C^s).
\]
If each $w_\ell:\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\to\R_+$ is bounded and nonincreasing, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-moment}
  \Tr\!\left[\Gamma(\tau)
    \prod_{\ell=1}^s w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell)\right]
  \leq
  \Tr[\Gamma(\tau)]
  \Tr\!\left[\tau^{(\gamma)}
    \prod_{\ell=1}^s w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell)\right].
\end{equation}
\end{propC}

To explain the factor $\Tr\Gamma(\tau)$ in
\eqref{eq:main-moment}, abbreviate
\[
  W=W(\widehat{\mathbf N})
  =\prod_{\ell=1}^s w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  c=\Tr\Gamma(\tau).
\]
The positive operator $\Gamma(\tau)$ has trace $c$.  If $c=1$, it is an
ordinary normalized output state.  If $0<c<1$, it is a subnormalized state.
Here the probabilistic operation is the map $\Gamma$ itself: it is completely
positive and trace-nonincreasing, and $c$ is its probability of success on the
input $\tau$.  Conditional on success, the normalized output is
\[
  \rho_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{\Gamma(\tau)}{c}.
\]
Substituting $\Gamma(\tau)=c\rho_{\mathrm{out}}$ into the two sides of
\eqref{eq:main-moment} gives
\[
  c\Tr[\rho_{\mathrm{out}}W]
  \leq c\Tr[\tau^{(\gamma)}W].
\]
After cancelling $c$, this is precisely the usual least-noise comparison
between two normalized states.  The word ``homogeneous'' refers only to this
common factor: replacing $\Gamma$ by $a\Gamma$ multiplies both sides of
\eqref{eq:main-moment} by the same number $a$.  If $c=0$, positivity gives
$\Gamma(\tau)=0$, and both sides are zero.

There is a second reason for allowing $c<1$ in Proposition C.3.  The maps
constructed later in Appendix C are weak-* limits of channels.  Such a limit
can lose trace.  For example,
\[
  \rho_j=|j\rangle\!\langle j|
  \quad\text{has}\quad \Tr\rho_j=1,
  \qquad
  \Tr(\rho_jK)\longrightarrow0
  \quad\text{for every compact }K.
\]
Thus $\rho_j$ converges weak-* to the zero operator, whose trace is zero: the
states move to higher and higher photon numbers, beyond what any fixed
compact observable detects.  The phrase ``trace lost at infinity'' refers
to exactly this phenomenon.  More generally, if channels $\Gamma_j$ converge
weak-* to $\Gamma$, then on the input $\tau$ one may have
\[
  \Tr\Gamma_j(\tau)=1\quad\text{for every }j,
  \qquad
  \Gamma_j(\tau)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}^*}\Gamma(\tau),
  \qquad
  c:=\Tr\Gamma(\tau)<1.
\]
For $c>0$, write $\Gamma(\tau)=c\rho_{\mathrm{out}}$, where
$\Tr\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=1$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:main-moment} then reads
\[
  \underbrace{\Tr[\Gamma(\tau)W]}_{c\Tr[\rho_{\mathrm{out}}W]}
  \leq
  \underbrace{\Tr\Gamma(\tau)}_{c}
  \Tr[\tau^{(\gamma)}W].
\]
Thus the two appearances of $c$ are exactly the two appearances of
$\Gamma(\tau)$ in \eqref{eq:main-moment}: once inside its weighted
expectation on the left and once through its trace on the right.

\section{Proof: the one-mode case}

\subsection*{Step 1: phase twirling changes neither side}

Let $R_\theta=e^{\mathrm i\theta\widehat N}$.  Replace $\Gamma$ by
\[
  \Gamma^{\mathrm{ph}}(X)
  =\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
    R_{-\theta}\Gamma(R_\theta XR_\theta^*)R_{-\theta}^*\,\mathrm d\theta.
\]
The thermal input and $w(\widehat N)$ commute with every $R_\theta$, so both
traces in \eqref{eq:main-moment} are unchanged.  The twirl preserves
complete positivity, trace nonincrease, and displacement covariance, and it
makes the map phase covariant.  Independent phase twirls are used in the
multimode case.

Phase covariance is what lets the proof speak about the output
photon-number law and makes the idler state diagonal in its number basis.

\subsection*{Step 2: covariance makes the success probability constant}

The positive output-trace functional has the form
\[
  \Tr\Gamma(X)=\Tr(AX),
  \qquad 0\leq A\leq I.
\]
The displacement covariance relation is
\[
  \Gamma\!\left(D(z)XD(z)^*\right)
  =D(\sqrt\gamma z)\Gamma(X)D(\sqrt\gamma z)^*.
\]
Taking the trace and using invariance of the trace under unitary conjugation
gives
\[
  \Tr\Gamma\!\left(D(z)XD(z)^*\right)
  =\Tr\Gamma(X).
\]
Using $\Tr\Gamma(Y)=\Tr(AY)$ on both sides and cyclicity of the trace, this
becomes
\[
  \Tr\!\left[A D(z)XD(z)^*\right]
  =\Tr(AX),
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \Tr\!\left[\bigl(D(z)^*AD(z)-A\bigr)X\right]=0
\]
for every trace-class operator $X$.  Therefore
\[
  D(z)^*AD(z)=A
  \qquad(z\in\C).
\]
The Weyl representation is irreducible, so Schur's lemma gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constant-trace}
  A=cI,
  \qquad
  \Tr\Gamma(X)=c\Tr X,
  \qquad 0\leq c\leq1.
\end{equation}

If $c=0$, positivity forces $\Gamma=0$ and the desired inequality is
trivial.  If $c>0$, the normalized map
\[
  \Lambda=c^{-1}\Gamma
\]
is a trace-preserving channel satisfying phase covariance and displacement
covariance with gain $\sqrt\gamma$.

\subsection*{Step 3: apply the least-noise stochastic theorem}

The displacement-covariant amplifier classification in
\href{https://www.maths.nottingham.ac.uk/plp/pmzmig/preprints/cloning.pdf}
{Sections II.C--D and III of Gu\c{t}\u{a}--Matsumoto} writes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idler-characterization}
  \Lambda^*(D(\xi))
  =\Tr[\sigma D(-\sqrt{\gamma-1}\,\bar\xi)]
    D(\sqrt\gamma\,\xi)
\end{equation}
for an idler state $\sigma$.  Phase covariance forces
$e^{\mathrm i\theta\widehat N}\sigma e^{-\mathrm i\theta\widehat N}=\sigma$
for every $\theta$, hence $\sigma=\sum_{k\geq0}s_k|k\rangle\langle k|$.
A two-mode-squeezing unitary realizes \(\Lambda\) by mixing the input mode
\(a_{\mathrm{in}}\) with an independent idler mode \(b_{\mathrm{idler}}\):
\[
  a_{\mathrm{out}}
  =\sqrt\gamma\,a_{\mathrm{in}}+\sqrt{\gamma-1}\,b_{\mathrm{idler}}^*.
\]
Consequently, for phase-invariant input and idler states,
\[
  \mathbb E N_{\mathrm{out}}
  =\gamma\,\mathbb E N_{\mathrm{in}}
   +(\gamma-1)(\mathbb E N_{\mathrm{idler}}+1),
\]
so the vacuum idler, for which $N_{\mathrm{idler}}=0$, clearly minimizes
the mean output photon number.  The result needed here is stronger than this
mean comparison.  The stochastic-order calculation in
\href{https://www.maths.nottingham.ac.uk/plp/pmzmig/preprints/cloning.pdf}
{Lemma 2.3 and Section III of Gu\c{t}\u{a}--Matsumoto} shows
that every nonzero idler occupation shifts the entire output law upward in
first-order stochastic order.  Since a diagonal idler state is a mixture of
number states, its output law also stochastically dominates the vacuum-idler
law $\tau_{q^{(\gamma)}}$.

For the thermal input, set
\[
  P_\Lambda(k)=\langle k|\Lambda(\tau_q)|k\rangle,
  \qquad
  P_\gamma(k)=(1-q^{(\gamma)})(q^{(\gamma)})^k.
\]
The arbitrary-gain stochastic-order theorem states precisely that the
vacuum-idler law $P_\gamma$ is stochastically smallest:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stochastic-order}
  P_\gamma\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}P_\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \sum_{k=0}^mP_\gamma(k)\geq
  \sum_{k=0}^mP_\Lambda(k)
  \quad(m\geq0).
\end{equation}
Thus ``smallest photon number'' refers to the whole distribution, not to a
deterministic photon number or a pointwise comparison of probabilities.
Summation by parts converts the cumulative inequalities in
\eqref{eq:stochastic-order} into the decreasing-test inequality: if
$F_P(m)=\sum_{k=0}^mP(k)$ and $w_\infty=\lim_mw(m)$, then
\[
  \sum_{k\geq0}P(k)w(k)
  =w_\infty+
   \sum_{m\geq0}\bigl(w(m)-w(m+1)\bigr)F_P(m).
\]
The term $w_\infty$ is the same for both laws because both have total mass
one.  All coefficients in the last sum are nonnegative, so
$F_{P_\gamma}\geq F_{P_\Lambda}$ gives
\[
  \Tr[\Lambda(\tau_q)w(\widehat N)]
  \leq
  \Tr[\tau_{q^{(\gamma)}}w(\widehat N)].
\]
Multiplying by $c=\Tr\Gamma(\tau_q)$ proves
\eqref{eq:main-moment} for one mode.

The stochastic minimality in this step is the specialized external input.
The phase twirl, constant-trace reduction, normalization, and conversion to
the conic inequality are internal parts of the proof.

\section{Proof: the multimode reduction}

One cannot simply multiply the one-mode inequalities because $\Gamma$ may
couple all modes.  Instead, the proof packages the other modes into a
weighted environment.

If one of the weights is identically zero, both sides of
\eqref{eq:main-moment} vanish.  Otherwise rescale each weight by its positive
supremum; by homogeneity it is enough to assume
\[
  0\leq w_\ell\leq1.
\]
Set
\[
  W_{>1}=\prod_{\ell=2}^s w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell),
  \qquad
  \tau_{>1}=\bigotimes_{\ell=2}^s\tau_{q_\ell},
\]
and define a one-mode map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effective-map}
  \Gamma_1(X)
  =\Tr_{2,\ldots,s}\!\left[
    (I\ot W_{>1}^{1/2})
    \Gamma(X\ot\tau_{>1})
    (I\ot W_{>1}^{1/2})
  \right].
\end{equation}

Every operation in \eqref{eq:effective-map} is completely positive.  Since
$0\leq W_{>1}\leq I$ and $\Gamma$ is trace nonincreasing, $\Gamma_1$ is
trace nonincreasing.  It inherits displacement covariance in the first
mode.  It also inherits phase covariance because the fixed thermal inputs
and $W_{>1}$ are phase invariant after the independent phase twirl.
Apply the one-mode result to the map $\Gamma_1$, the first-mode thermal input
$\tau_{q_1}$, and the first-mode test $w_1(\widehat N_1)$.  It gives
\[
  \Tr\!\left[\Gamma_1(\tau_{q_1})w_1(\widehat N_1)\right]
  \leq
  \Tr\Gamma_1(\tau_{q_1})
  \Tr\!\left[\tau_{q_1^{(\gamma)}}w_1(\widehat N_1)\right].
\]
The left side is the original weighted expression, because
\begin{align*}
  \Tr\!\left[\Gamma_1(\tau_{q_1})w_1(\widehat N_1)\right]
  &=\Tr\!\left[
      \Gamma(\tau_{q_1}\ot\tau_{>1})
      \bigl(w_1(\widehat N_1)\ot W_{>1}\bigr)
    \right] \\
  &=\Tr[\Gamma(\tau)W].
\end{align*}
Here the first equality follows from the definition of the partial trace and
cyclicity of the trace.  Similarly, omitting the first-mode test gives
\[
  \Tr\Gamma_1(\tau_{q_1})
  =\Tr\!\left[
    \Gamma(\tau)(I\ot W_{>1})
  \right]
  =\Tr[\Gamma(\tau)W_{>1}].
\]
Substituting these two identities into the one-mode bound yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:peel-first}
  \Tr[\Gamma(\tau)W]
  \leq
  \Tr[\tau_{q_1^{(\gamma)}}w_1(\widehat N_1)]
  \Tr[\Gamma(\tau)W_{>1}].
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:peel-first} removes the first weight and replaces it by
the corresponding quantum-limited expectation.  Repeat the same construction
for modes $2,\ldots,s$.  After the last mode, the remaining term is
$\Tr\Gamma(\tau)$, while the extracted scalar factors multiply to
\[
  \prod_{\ell=1}^s
  \Tr[\tau_{q_\ell^{(\gamma)}}w_\ell(\widehat N_\ell)]
  =\Tr[\tau^{(\gamma)}W].
\]
This is precisely \eqref{eq:main-moment}.

\section{The particular witness used later}

For the orbital Gaussian model, let
\[
  \tau=\sum_{\mathbf k}t_{\mathbf k}\ketbra{\mathbf k},
  \qquad
  \tau^{(\gamma)}
  =\sum_{\mathbf k}t_{\mathbf k}^{(\gamma)}\ketbra{\mathbf k}.
\]
Here $\mathbf k=(k_{ij})_{i<j}$ ranges over
$\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{r_d}$, and $\widehat N_{ij}$ denotes the number operator
of the mode indexed by $i<j$.  Its total occupation number is
$|\mathbf k|=\sum_{i<j}k_{ij}$.
Appendix C introduces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Wgamma}
  W_\gamma
  =\sum_{\mathbf k}
    \sqrt{\frac{t_{\mathbf k}}{t_{\mathbf k}^{(\gamma)}}}
    \ketbra{\mathbf k}.
\end{equation}
Because both thermal laws are products,
\[
  \sqrt{\frac{t_{\mathbf k}}{t_{\mathbf k}^{(\gamma)}}}
  =\prod_{i<j}
  \sqrt{\frac{1-q_{ij}}{1-q_{ij}^{(\gamma)}}}
  \left(\frac{q_{ij}}{q_{ij}^{(\gamma)}}\right)^{k_{ij}/2}.
\]
Each factor is positive, bounded, and nonincreasing because
$q_{ij}<q_{ij}^{(\gamma)}$.  The eigenvalues tend to zero as
$|\mathbf k|\to\infty$, so $W_\gamma$ is compact and injective.
Proposition C.3 applies to this witness, and direct calculation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:witness-identities}
  \Tr(\tau^{(\gamma)}W_\gamma)
  =\Tr(\tau W_\gamma^{-1})
  =\sum_{\mathbf k}\sqrt{t_{\mathbf k}t_{\mathbf k}^{(\gamma)}}
  =\Borb(\gamma,p).
\end{equation}
Thus one witness simultaneously matches the weighted fidelity inequality,
the compact-limit topology, and the least-noise moment bound.

\section{Dependencies and role in the paper}

The logical core is
\[
  \begin{gathered}
  \text{Weyl irreducibility and one-mode least-noise stochastic order}\\
  {}+\text{mode-by-mode reduction}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Proposition C.3}.
  \end{gathered}
\]
The orbital specialization of Proposition C.5 applies it to the exactly
covariant limit of F\o lner-averaged channels.  The full classical--quantum
specialization applies it to a classically weighted quantum limit after first
proving that the limit is trace nonincreasing.  In both cases,
\eqref{eq:witness-identities} converts the moment estimate into the factor
$\Borb(\gamma,p)$.
