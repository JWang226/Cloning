\begin{abstract}
Lemma C.5 identifies every positive translation-covariant map from
$L^1(\mathsf H)$ to finite measures.  Such a map must first dilate the input
location by $\sqrt\gamma$ and then add noise with a fixed finite measure
$\mu$.  The proof obtains $\mu$ by feeding the map an approximate point mass
at the origin.  This note explains the covariance convention, the weak-*
extraction, and the passage from approximate point masses to arbitrary
$L^1$ inputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{The statement in probabilistic language}

Here $\Hc$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space with Lebesgue measure
$\dd x$, and $E\subseteq\Hc$ always denotes a Borel set.  If
$u\geq0$ and $\int u(x)\dd x=1$, then $u(x)\dd x$ is the law of an input
random variable $X$.  The symbol $\mathcal R$ is not a random variable: it
is a positive linear map, and
\[
  \nu_u:=\mathcal R(u)
\]
is the finite measure produced from the input density $u$.  If
$\nu_u(\Hc)=1$, one may choose an output random variable $Y$ with law
$\nu_u$, so that $\Pr(Y\in E)=\nu_u(E)$.  If
$c_u:=\nu_u(\Hc)<1$, then $\nu_u$ is instead a subprobability measure; it
can be represented by adjoining a failure point $\partial\notin\Hc$ and
setting
\[
  \Pr(Y\in E)=\nu_u(E),
  \qquad
  \Pr(Y=\partial)=1-c_u.
\]

To state covariance precisely, let $T_a(y):=y+a$.  Translating a finite
measure $\nu$ by $a$ means taking its pushforward
\[
  (T_a)_\#\nu(E)
  :=\nu(T_a^{-1}(E))
  =\nu(E-a),
  \qquad E-a:=\{y-a:y\in E\}.
\]
If $\nu$ is the law of $Y$, then $(T_a)_\#\nu$ is the law of $Y+a$.
Likewise, $u(\,\cdot-h)$ is the density of $X+h$.  Hence the covariance
assumption can be written equivalently as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal R(u(\,\cdot-h))
  &=(T_{\sqrt\gamma h})_\#\mathcal R(u),\\
  \mathcal R(u(\,\cdot-h))(E)
  &=\mathcal R(u)(E-\sqrt\gamma h).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus replacing the input law of $X$ by that of $X+h$ replaces the output
measure $\nu_u$ by its translate through $\sqrt\gamma h$; when the measures
are probability laws, this says that the new output has the same law as
$Y+\sqrt\gamma h$.

Lemma C.5 says that there is one finite positive measure $\mu$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
  \mathcal R(u)(E)
  =\int_{\Hc}u(x)\,\mu(E-\sqrt\gamma x)\dd x.
\end{equation}
For each input point $x$, define the finite measure
\[
  K(x,E):=\mu(E-\sqrt\gamma x).
\]
Then \eqref{eq:kernel} says that $\mathcal R(u)$ is the mixture of the
pointwise output measures $K(x,\,\cdot)$ with weights $u(x)\dd x$.  If
$\mu(\Hc)=1$, each $K(x,\,\cdot)$ is a probability measure and may be read
as the conditional law of the output given $X=x$:
\[
  K(x,E)=\Pr(Y\in E\mid X=x).
\]
Indeed, if $Z\sim\mu$ is independent of $X$ and
$Y:=\sqrt\gamma X+Z$, then
\[
  \Pr(Y\in E\mid X=x)
  =\mu(E-\sqrt\gamma x)=K(x,E),
\]
and averaging the conditional measures over $X$ gives
\[
  \Pr(Y\in E)
  =\int_{\Hc}u(x)K(x,E)\dd x
  =\mathcal R(u)(E).
\]

The reason covariance forces the noise measure to be independent of $x$ is
most transparent at the level of point inputs.  Temporarily suppose that
$\delta_x$ were an admissible input and denote its output measure by $K_x$;
this notation does not assume the desired representation.  Covariance would
give $K_{x+h}(E)=K_x(E-\sqrt\gamma h)$.  Recenter $K_x$ by defining
\[
  \mu_x:=(T_{-\sqrt\gamma x})_\#K_x,
  \qquad
  \mu_x(E)=K_x(E+\sqrt\gamma x).
\]
If $K_x$ is a probability law, then $\mu_x$ is precisely the law of the
residual $Y-\sqrt\gamma x$.  The covariance identity now gives
\[
  \mu_{x+h}(E)
  =K_{x+h}(E+\sqrt\gamma(x+h))
  =K_x(E+\sqrt\gamma x)
  =\mu_x(E).
\]
Since $h$ is arbitrary, all the recentered measures $\mu_x$ coincide.
Point masses do not belong to $L^1(\Hc)$, so the proof makes this heuristic
rigorous by approximating $\delta_0$, extracting its output limit $\mu$,
and extending the formula to all $L^1$ inputs by convolution.

Finally, if $c:=\mu(\Hc)<1$, then $K(x,\Hc)=c$ for every $x$ and
$\mathcal R(u)(\Hc)=c$ for every normalized $u$.  When $0<c<1$, this may be
realized by taking a Bernoulli variable $S$ with $\Pr(S=1)=c$, an independent
noise variable $Z$ with law $\mu/c$, and declaring the output to be
$\sqrt\gamma X+Z$ when $S=1$ and $\partial$ when $S=0$.  In the application,
the missing mass $1-c$ records probability that may have escaped to
infinity in the weak-* limit.

\section{Functional-analytic setting}

The output space is
\[
  C_0(\Hc)^*,
\]
which the Riesz representation theorem identifies with the finite Radon
measures on $\Hc$.  The dual pairing of a test function $f$ with a measure
$\nu$ is
\[
  \langle f,\nu\rangle:=\int_{\Hc}f(y)\,\nu(\dd y).
\]
When $\nu$ is the law of a random variable $Y$, this pairing is
$\mathbb E[f(Y)]$; for a subprobability measure, the same interpretation
holds after setting $f(\partial)=0$ at the failure point.  Weak-* convergence
of measures means
\[
  \int f\dd\mu_j\longrightarrow\int f\dd\mu
  \qquad(f\in C_0(\Hc)).
\]
Thus weak-* convergence asserts convergence of these test-function averages,
not pointwise convergence of random variables.

The map
\[
  \mathcal R:L^1(\Hc)\longrightarrow C_0(\Hc)^*
\]
is a positive contraction.  Hence, for $u\geq0$,
\[
  \|\mathcal R(u)\|\leq\|u\|_1.
\]
For a positive measure its norm equals its total mass, so a normalized
nonnegative input produces an output measure of mass at most one.

\section{Statement of Lemma C.5}

\begin{lemmaC}
Let $\mathcal R:L^1(\Hc)\to C_0(\Hc)^*$ be a positive contraction satisfying
\eqref{eq:covariance}.  Then a finite positive measure $\mu$ with
$\mu(\Hc)\leq1$ exists such that \eqref{eq:kernel} holds for every
$u\in L^1(\Hc)$ and every Borel set $E$.
\end{lemmaC}

\section{Proof walkthrough}

\subsection*{Step 1: probe the kernel at the origin}

Choose a nonnegative approximate identity $u_j\in C_c(\Hc)$:
\[
  \int_{\Hc}u_j(x)\dd x=1,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{supp}(u_j)\longrightarrow\{0\}.
\]
The measures $u_j(x)\dd x$ are probability laws concentrating at the input
point $0$, so they approximate the point mass $\delta_0$.  Heuristically,
the output produced by $\delta_0$ is the noise measure; since $\delta_0$ is
not an $L^1$ density, use instead the sequence of output measures
\[
  \mu_j:=\mathcal R(u_j).
\]
Thus $\mu_j$ is a measure, not a random variable; when it has mass one, one
may represent it as the law of an output random variable.  Positivity and
contractivity imply $\mu_j(\Hc)\leq1$.

The unit ball of $C_0(\Hc)^*$ is weak-* compact.  Since $C_0(\Hc)$ is
separable, a subsequence converges weak-* to a positive measure $\mu$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu-limit}
  \int f\dd\mu_j\longrightarrow\int f\dd\mu
  \qquad(f\in C_0(\Hc)).
\end{equation}
The norm is weak-* lower semicontinuous, so $\mu(\Hc)\leq1$.  Equality need
not hold because the constant function $1$ does not belong to $C_0(\Hc)$
when $\Hc$ is noncompact.

\subsection*{Step 2: smooth an arbitrary input}

Use the convolution convention
\[
  (u*u_j)(y)=\int_{\Hc}u(x)u_j(y-x)\dd x
  =\int_{\Hc}u(x)u_j(\,\cdot-x)(y)\dd x.
\]
For normalized nonnegative $u$, if $X$ has density $u$ and $V_j$ has density
$u_j$ independently of $X$, then $u*u_j$ is the density of $X+V_j$.
Equivalently, it mixes the translated densities $u_j(\,\cdot-x)$ with
weights $u(x)\dd x$; the proof then extends the identity to arbitrary
$u\in L^1(\Hc)$ by linearity.
The approximate-identity property gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L1-approx}
  u*u_j\longrightarrow u
  \qquad\text{in }L^1(\Hc).
\end{equation}
Bounded linearity of $\mathcal R$ therefore implies
\[
  \mathcal R(u*u_j)\longrightarrow\mathcal R(u)
\]
in the norm of finite measures.

Linearity sends this mixture of input densities to the corresponding mixture
of output measures, and covariance translates the output of
$u_j(\,\cdot-x)$ by $\sqrt\gamma x$.  Therefore
\begin{align}
  \mathcal R(u*u_j)(E)
  &=\int_{\Hc}u(x)\,
    \mathcal R(u_j(\,\cdot-x))(E)\dd x\notag\\
  &=\int_{\Hc}u(x)\,
    \mathcal R(u_j)(E-\sqrt\gamma x)\dd x.
  \label{eq:smoothed-kernel}
\end{align}
Thus the desired convolution formula already holds for the smoothed input
$u*u_j$, with the finite measure $\mu_j=\mathcal R(u_j)$ in place of $\mu$.

\subsection*{Step 3: pass to the weak-* limit}

To justify the limit in \eqref{eq:smoothed-kernel}, pair both sides with an
arbitrary $f\in C_0(\Hc)$.  The right side becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test-pairing}
  \int_{\Hc}u(x)
  \left(\int_{\Hc}f(r+\sqrt\gamma x)\,\mu_j(\dd r)\right)\dd x.
\end{equation}
Here $r$ is the integration variable for the measure $\mu_j$; if $\mu_j$
is a probability law and $Z_j\sim\mu_j$, the inner integral is
$\mathbb E[f(\sqrt\gamma x+Z_j)]$.
For each fixed $x$, the translated function
$r\mapsto f(r+\sqrt\gamma x)$ still belongs to $C_0(\Hc)$, so
\eqref{eq:mu-limit} gives convergence of the inner integral to the same
expression with $\mu$.  Its absolute value is bounded by $\|f\|_\infty$, and
$|u|$ is integrable.  Dominated convergence therefore turns
\eqref{eq:test-pairing} into
\[
  \int_{\Hc}u(x)
  \left(\int_{\Hc}f(r+\sqrt\gamma x)\,\mu(\dd r)\right)\dd x.
\]
On the other side, norm convergence of the output measures gives
\[
  \int f\,\dd\mathcal R(u*u_j)
  \longrightarrow
  \int f\,\dd\mathcal R(u).
\]
The two limiting test-function integrals are therefore equal for every
$f\in C_0(\Hc)$.  Since these functions separate finite Radon measures,
the measure identity \eqref{eq:kernel} follows.

\section{Examples}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $\mu=\delta_0$, then
  \[
    \mathcal R(u)(E)=\int u(x)\mathbf1_E(\sqrt\gamma x)\dd x,
  \]
  so for $X\sim u(x)\dd x$ the output variable is
  $Y=\sqrt\gamma X$.
  \item If $\mu$ is a probability measure and $Z\sim\mu$ is independent of
  $X$, then the output measure $\mathcal R(u)$ is the law of
  $Y=\sqrt\gamma X+Z$.
  \item If $\mu(\Hc)=c<1$, then $\mathcal R(u)$ is a subprobability measure
  of mass $c$ for every normalized $u$; in the failure-point representation,
  the output lies in $\Hc$ with probability $c$ and equals $\partial$ with
  probability $1-c$.
\end{enumerate}

No density for $\mu$ is required.  The noise may have atoms or a singular
part, which is important because weak-* limits of absolutely continuous
output measures need not remain absolutely continuous.

\section{Role in Proposition C.6}

In the hybrid converse, $\mathcal R$ is the limiting classical trace kernel
of F\o lner-averaged hybrid channels.  Lemma C.5 isolates all its freedom in
the one measure $\mu$.  The classical Gaussian witness then yields
\[
  \int c_\gamma(r)\,\mu(\dd r)
  \leq B_{\mathrm{cl}}(\gamma,d)\mu(\Hc)
  \leq B_{\mathrm{cl}}(\gamma,d).
\]
That bound controls the trace of the weighted quantum limit and supplies the
classical factor in the final hybrid fidelity bound.
